# Drafts — Six Words, One Story

Every line below is exactly six words (counted, not guessed). Each has a one-line verdict:
does it stand as a whole story, and where does the turn land?

---

1. **His lunchbox came home still full.**
   Whole story: yes — a man leaves for work and only his lunchbox returns. Turn in the last two words, "still full", which turns an ordinary object into an absence.

2. **Mum hid letters. Dad never asked.**
   Whole story: yes — concealment, then a deliberate silence. Turn on "never asked": the second sentence rewrites the first as a shared secret.

3. **She learned his name on television.**
   Whole story: yes — a woman discovers who her man really was. Turn on "on television": the medium implies he is gone or exposed.

4. **He proposed; she asked about rent.**
   Whole story: yes — a proposal refused without a single refusal. Turn on "rent": the answer is arithmetic, not love.

5. **Every Sunday she visited. He forgot.**
   Whole story: yes — devotion and decline in six words. Turn on "forgot": the visits are being erased as they happen.

6. **The kettle boiled. Nobody poured. Again.**
   Whole story: yes — a household after a death, repeated daily. Turn on "Again", which moves the loss from event to routine.

7. **She kept his razor. It rusted.**
   Whole story: yes — a grief that outlasts its object. Turn on "rusted": time passes and the keeping becomes pointless, which is the point.

8. **The will named only the dog.**
   Whole story: yes — a family cut out with one legal sentence. Turn on "only the dog": the joke lands and then stops being funny.

9. **She married his brother. He toasted.**
   Whole story: yes — betrayal accepted publicly. Turn on "toasted": he attends, drinks, and consents, which is worse than anger.

10. **He watered her plants. They drowned.**
    Whole story: yes — care performed after the person is gone. Turn on "drowned": too much attention, too late, is another kind of neglect.

11. **The interview went well. Nobody called.**
    Whole story: yes — hope, then the ordinary cruelty of silence. Turn on "Nobody called": "went well" is retrospectively exposed as self-flattery.

12. **Three months sober. She still left.**
    Whole story: yes — a repair that arrives too late. Turn on "still": the fix is real and insufficient.

13. **He fixed the sink. She left.**
    Whole story: borderline — the shape is a setup-punchline joke rather than a life. Turn on "She left", but it is the same turn as draft 12 with less at stake. Cut.

14. **Two plates, two cups. One washed.**
    Whole story: no — a list of objects with a count attached. The "turn" is arithmetic, not consequence. Fails the first test outright.

15. **Buy one, get one free. Forever.**
    Whole story: no — a slogan wearing a full stop. Cleverness for its own sake; nothing is implied and no one is in it. Cut.

16. **Sign here. Initial there. Thank you.**
    Whole story: no — a fragment of dialogue with no person, no stake, and no consequence. It documents a procedure; it does not tell a story. Cut.

Rejected before drafting: the shoe-shop classified ad, and anything near it. It is the form's most quoted example, and a judge has read it a thousand times; a story that reminds them of it loses on familiarity alone.

---

## Why #1 was kept

It satisfies the tests in order.

*Complete story.* A subject is implied (the person who packs the lunchbox, or the one who carries it), an action occurs (the lunchbox comes home), and a consequence arrives in the final two words. Nothing is missing from the arc; only the explanation is.

*The turn.* "Came home" is warm and routine, and it quietly does the work of a person — the box travels a route its owner no longer does. Then "still full" closes it: the food was never eaten. The reader supplies the rest without being told: an accident, a shift that ended early, a man who did not come back. The story never says the word death.

*Economy.* Seven words would spoil it. "His lunchbox came home again still full" adds nothing; "untouched" instead of "still full" is a synonym with a fancier hat. Each word carries either an object, a motion, or the turn.

*Aloud.* Two clean stresses, "lunchbox" and "still full", and no consonant pile-ups. It is a sentence a person can say in one breath.

*What is left unsaid.* Everything that matters — who he was, what happened, who is holding the box. The reader builds the funeral from a lunchbox.

The competing finalists were #2 and #5. Both are strong, but #2 needs the reader to infer an entire affair from a plural noun, and #5 leans on a single clinical word. #1 lets an ordinary object do the telling, which is the harder and better trick: it is a domestic, working-life story, and the last two words do all the damage.
